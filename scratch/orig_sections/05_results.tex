\section{Results and Analysis}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Convergence on balanced held-out dev}
Table~\ref{tab:dev_curve} tracks validation over three epochs on the separated Hermes dev (1,239 targets) and xLAM dev (4,145 targets) splits. Tool selection saturates in Epoch~1 ($H_1$ \texttt{tool\_acc} of 0.999 on Hermes and 0.991 on xLAM; 1.000 and 0.992 by Epoch~3). Both corpora improve monotonically, lifting macro dev \texttt{exact\_call} from 0.857 (Epoch~1) to 0.868 (Epoch~2) and \textbf{0.874} (Epoch~3, restored as best) while training loss falls from 1.503 to 0.287. On the disjoint mid-training probe (559 targets), \texttt{exact\_call} climbs from 0.364 at optimizer step 2,000 to 0.802 at step 10,000, peaking at \textbf{0.882} at step 30,000 and holding at 0.856--0.869 through step 42,000.

\subsection{Headline fair-eval and multi-LLM router comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:headline} compares \textsc{SpanCall}, two hosted LLMs (\texttt{google/gemma-4-26b-a4b-it} and \texttt{openai/gpt-oss-120b}), and the production \textbf{Router} (\textsc{SpanCall} + LLM fallback) on the 1:1 matched Tier~1 benchmark ($n{=}1{,}735, n_F{=}1{,}277$ per corpus).

With 1:1 balanced training, \textsc{SpanCall} performs consistently across both corpora: \textbf{0.743} \texttt{exact\_F} on Hermes eval and \textbf{0.766} on xLAM eval (\textbf{0.755} macro average, 0.801 macro \texttt{param\_F1}) at \textbf{77.0\,ms} macro mean latency (p50 48.1\,ms, \textbf{$38.8\times$--$91.3\times$ faster} than the two LLMs) with zero generation tokens. Paired 10,000-resample bootstrap 95\% CIs confirm that \textsc{SpanCall} alone beats \texttt{gemma-4-26b} by \textbf{+35.3\,pp} ($[+31.3, +39.3]$) and \texttt{gpt-oss-120b} by \textbf{+32.3\,pp} ($[+28.4, +36.4]$) on Hermes, while statistically tying both LLMs on xLAM ($-1.2$\,pp $[-4.2, +1.9]$ vs.\ Gemma~4; $-1.1$\,pp $[-4.1, +2.0]$ vs.\ \texttt{gpt-oss-120b}).

Deploying \textsc{SpanCall} as an abstention-gated fast path (\textbf{Router}) outperforms \emph{both} \textsc{SpanCall} alone and the LLM alone on every corpus: \textbf{Router (Gemma~4)} reaches 0.808 macro \texttt{exact\_F} (0.801 Hermes, 0.815 xLAM; +41.1\,pp and +3.8\,pp $[+1.0, +6.6]$ over Gemma~4 alone), and \textbf{Router (\texttt{gpt-120b})} reaches 0.807 macro \texttt{exact\_F} (0.800 Hermes, 0.814 xLAM; +38.0\,pp and +3.7\,pp $[+0.9, +6.4]$ over \texttt{gpt-120b} alone). Both routers invoke the LLM on only \textbf{17.6\%} of turns, saving \textbf{79.6\%--80.1\%} of total tokens and cutting mean latency by \textbf{$4.5\times$--$5.2\times$} (median latency \textbf{56.9\,ms} vs.\ 1,112.8--6,414.1\,ms).

Adding the deterministic post-$H_1$ schema-type gate (\textbf{Router+Schema}) delivers a second major jump: \textbf{Router+Schema (Gemma~4)} reaches \textbf{0.857} macro \texttt{exact\_F} (\textbf{0.825} Hermes, \textbf{0.889} xLAM; +43.5\,pp $[+40.3, +46.8]$ and \textbf{+11.1\,pp} $[+8.9, +13.4]$ over Gemma~4 alone, and +2.4\,pp $[+1.1, +3.8]$ / +7.4\,pp $[+5.9, +8.9]$ over abstention-only Router), and \textbf{Router+Schema (\texttt{gpt-120b})} reaches \textbf{0.855} macro \texttt{exact\_F} (0.821 Hermes, \textbf{0.889} xLAM; +40.1\,pp $[+36.8, +43.3]$ and \textbf{+11.2\,pp} $[+9.0, +13.4]$ over \texttt{gpt-120b} alone, and +2.1\,pp $[+0.8, +3.4]$ / +7.5\,pp $[+6.0, +9.1]$ over abstention-only Router). It invokes the LLM on \textbf{34.3\%} of turns (23.3\% schema-routed, 11.0\% abstention), still saving \textbf{59.7\%--59.9\%} of tokens and cutting mean latency by \textbf{$2.6\times$--$2.7\times$} (median \textbf{66.4\,ms}), while also slightly lowering \texttt{false\_hit\_on\_A} (0.956 and 0.936 vs.\ 0.975 and 0.965).

\subsection{Call-resolution funnel and error breakdown}
\label{sec:results:funnel}
To see where each system succeeds or loses accuracy, Table~\ref{tab:funnel} decomposes Stratum~F ($n_F{=}1{,}277$ per corpus) into four cumulative stages---commitment (\texttt{hitF}), right tool (\texttt{tool}), right parameter keys (\texttt{keys}), and strict end-to-end exact hit (\texttt{exHit}, requiring both commitment and exact values)---together with precision at hit (\texttt{P@hit}${=}\texttt{exHit}/\texttt{hitF}$) and entity-swap counts (\texttt{swap}).

Before gating, \textsc{SpanCall}'s tool head ($H_1$) selects the correct tool on \textbf{99.9\%} of Hermes rows (1,276/1,277) and \textbf{99.6\%} of xLAM rows (1,272/1,277). After gating, \textsc{SpanCall} commits on 87.9\% (Hermes) and 88.3\% (xLAM) of Stratum~F rows, keeps exact parameter keys on 87.2\% and 80.6\%, and delivers \textbf{0.806} and \textbf{0.824} \texttt{P@hit}---higher precision upon commitment than \emph{both} LLMs on \emph{both} corpora (0.543 on Hermes; 0.804--0.812 on xLAM). Note that 44 Hermes and 50 xLAM rows have exact extracted payloads that are conservatively gated off (\texttt{hit=False}), accounting for the small gap between \texttt{exact\_F} in Table~\ref{tab:headline} and strict \texttt{exHit}; under the Router those abstentions fall back to the LLM, pushing strict \texttt{exHit} to 0.800--0.801 (Hermes) and 0.814--0.815 (xLAM). Adding the post-$H_1$ schema gate (\textbf{R+Schema}) cuts wrong-value errors from 226 to \textbf{158--162} on Hermes and from 202--206 to \textbf{107--110} on xLAM, pushing \texttt{P@hit} to \textbf{0.841--0.847} (Hermes) and \textbf{0.895--0.898} (xLAM). Finally, exclusivity-constrained DFS eliminates duplicate-span collapse (0 on every split) and cuts entity swaps by \textbf{$19\times$ on Hermes} (4 vs.\ 76--79) and \textbf{$4\times$--$5\times$ on xLAM} (34 vs.\ 136--162).

\subsection{Where extraction helps and where it stops}
\label{sec:results:complexity}
Table~\ref{tab:complexity} partitions Tier~1 calls into \textsc{Normal} (all-scalar) vs.\ \textsc{Complex} (\texttt{array}/\texttt{object}). On \textsc{Normal} calls ($n_F{=}1{,}108$ per corpus), \textsc{SpanCall} beats \emph{both} LLMs on \emph{both} corpora: \textbf{0.798} vs.\ 0.360/0.397 on Hermes (\textbf{+40.1--43.8\,pp}) and \textbf{0.840} vs.\ 0.755/0.755 on xLAM (\textbf{+8.5\,pp}). Pairing \textsc{SpanCall} with LLM fallback pushes \textsc{Normal} \texttt{exact\_F} further to \textbf{0.861--0.864} on Hermes and \textbf{0.884} on xLAM.

On \textsc{Complex} calls ($n_F{=}169$ per corpus), \textsc{SpanCall} drops to 0.385 (Hermes) and 0.278 (xLAM), whereas the LLMs lead decisively (0.568--0.586 on Hermes; \textbf{0.923} for both LLMs on xLAM). Crucially, \textsc{SpanCall} still commits on 90.5\%--92.3\% of complex calls (\texttt{hit\_F}), which explains why the abstention-gated Router only reaches 0.355--0.396 on complex calls: because the extractor fires confidently with a wrong array/object payload, those rows rarely fall back.

\textbf{Router+Schema} resolves this cleanly. By checking only the $H_1$-selected tool $\hat{t}$'s declared schema (zero ground-truth access) and routing complex $\hat{t}$ schemas directly to the LLM, \textbf{Router+Schema} recovers \textbf{100\%} of the LLM's complex-call accuracy (0.586/0.568 on Hermes; \textbf{0.923} on xLAM) while preserving \textsc{SpanCall}'s scalar lead (0.859--0.862 Hermes, \textbf{0.884} xLAM). Performing this check \emph{post-$H_1$} rather than pre-encode is essential: a naive pre-encode gate that routes to the LLM whenever \emph{any} candidate tool in the pool has a complex parameter is triggered by complex distractors, raising LLM calls from 34.3\% to 46.3\%, dropping Hermes \textsc{Normal} from 0.862 to 0.798, and cutting macro \texttt{exact\_F} from 0.855--0.857 down to 0.810--0.812. OOD splits follow the exact same scalar-vs.-complex split: 0.770 vs.\ 0.392 on Hermes OOD-Schema, 0.781 vs.\ 0.379 on Hermes OOD-Para, and \textbf{0.822} vs.\ 0.330 on xLAM OOD-Para.

\subsection{Offline refusal sweep and abstention gap}
\label{sec:results:refusal}
Abstention on ungroundable turns remains the primary safety gap: \texttt{false\_hit\_on\_A} is 0.638 on Hermes eval and 0.690 on xLAM eval (still 19--26 points below the zero-shot LLMs at 0.879--0.895, though the Router inherits the LLM's false-hit rate on abstained turns, reaching 0.965--0.975, and 0.936--0.956 under \textbf{Router+Schema}). Table~\ref{tab:refusal_sweep} sweeps whole-call confidence and margin thresholds offline over committed Stratum~F predictions on Hermes eval (1,123 committed: 905 correct, 218 wrong). Raising $\tau_{\text{conf}}$ to 0.70 removes 186 predictions at a \textbf{2.15:1} bad-to-good ratio (127 wrong vs.\ 59 correct), confirming that scalar confidence separates errors usefully on Hermes while remaining insufficient for zero-false-hit abstention alone (on xLAM the ratio stays near 0.94:1 because xLAM errors are concentrated in confident complex-type misses).

\subsection{Full ID, OOD, and adversarial generalization}
Table~\ref{tab:ood} evaluates \textsc{SpanCall} across all eight Tier~2 full splits (34,969 rows total). Generalization to unseen schemas and paraphrases is strong across both corpora: \textbf{0.711} \texttt{exact\_F} on Hermes OOD-Schema ($n_F{=}1{,}204$), \textbf{0.735} on Hermes OOD-Para ($n_F{=}1{,}341$), \textbf{0.799} on xLAM OOD-Schema ($n_F{=}5{,}010$), and \textbf{0.793} on xLAM OOD-Para ($n_F{=}6{,}868$), all at 38.7--127.6\,ms mean latency (p50 24.7--72.6\,ms). These numbers match or exceed the full ID eval splits (0.708 Hermes, 0.757 xLAM), confirming that anchor-conditioned extraction generalizes to novel schemas rather than memorizing training tools. On the adversarial splits (no Stratum~F, post-sanitization non-injection adversarial turns), \texttt{false\_hit\_on\_A} remains high (0.816 Hermes, 0.788 xLAM), underscoring that semantic abstention requires dedicated negative training or an explicit abstain head.

\subsection{Candidate-pool size is not the bottleneck}
Table~\ref{tab:buckets} slices Tier~1 by candidate tool count $|\text{tools}|$, including the tool-count-controlled \texttt{1--5} shared regime. On xLAM, \textsc{SpanCall} stays completely flat from 1--2 tools (0.777) through 3--5 (0.758) and 6--10 (0.758), matching both LLMs across every bucket while the Router leads at 0.812--0.836 on 1--5 tools. On Hermes, 1--2 tools reach 0.782 (Router 0.841--0.844 vs.\ 0.360--0.386 LLM), while the small 3--5 tool slice ($n_F{=}112$) is dominated by complex schemas where LLMs lead (0.705--0.768 vs.\ 0.339). Across the shared 1--5 tool regime, \textsc{SpanCall} achieves 0.743 (Hermes) and 0.767 (xLAM), confirming that two-step re-encoding neutralizes schema cardinality and that argument type (\S\ref{sec:results:complexity}) is the true boundary. Under \textbf{Router+Schema}, xLAM climbs to \textbf{0.875--0.906} across every tool-count bucket (0.887--0.890 on shared 1--5) and Hermes 3--5 tools jumps from 0.357--0.366 to 0.500--0.509.
